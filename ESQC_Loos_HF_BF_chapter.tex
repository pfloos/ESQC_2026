\section{Hartree--Fock theory}

\subsection{The electronic problem}
\subsubsection{Motivations}

We consider the \red{time-independent} Schr\"odinger equation and seek
stationary electronic states. Hartree--Fock (HF) theory provides an
\red{ab initio} description: apart from the choice of basis set, the method
contains no empirical parameters. Throughout this chapter, relativistic
effects are neglected.

The central approximation is a \red{mean-field} treatment of the
electron--electron interaction. Each electron moves in the average field
generated by all the others. This replacement does not remove the
electron--electron interaction, but it restricts the form in which that
interaction enters the wavefunction. Despite this restriction, HF theory is
the starting point for much of electronic-structure theory.

\subsubsection{The Born--Oppenheimer approximation}

For a molecule containing $N$ electrons and $M$ nuclei, the molecular
wavefunction depends on the electronic coordinates $\{\br_i\}$ and nuclear
coordinates $\{\bR_A\}$. It satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \chH \Phi(\{\br_i\},\{\bR_A\})
  = \cE \Phi(\{\br_i\},\{\bR_A\}).
\end{equation}
The total Hamiltonian contains five contributions,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \chH
  = \green{\chT_\text{n}}
  + \blue{\chT_\text{e}}
  + \orange{\chV_\text{ne}}
  + \red{\chV_\text{ee}}
  + \violet{\chV_\text{nn}}
  }.
  \label{eq:total-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
Here, $\green{\chT_\text{n}}$ and $\blue{\chT_\text{e}}$ are the nuclear
and electronic kinetic-energy operators. The operator
$\orange{\chV_\text{ne}}$ describes the Coulomb attraction between nuclei
and electrons, while $\red{\chV_\text{ee}}$ and
$\violet{\chV_\text{nn}}$ describe the electron--electron and
nucleus--nucleus Coulomb repulsions.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{coord}
  \caption{Electronic and nuclear coordinates entering the molecular
  Hamiltonian.}
  \label{fig:molecular-coordinates}
\end{figure}

In atomic units, for which $m=e=\hbar=1$, these terms read
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
  \green{\chT_\text{n}}
  &= -\sum_{A=1}^{M}\frac{\laplacian_A}{2M_A},
  &
  \blue{\chT_\text{e}}
  &= -\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\laplacian_i}{2},
  \\
  \orange{\chV_\text{ne}}
  &= -\sum_{A=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{Z_A}{r_{iA}},
  &
  \red{\chV_\text{ee}}
  &= \sum_{i<j}^{N}\frac{1}{r_{ij}},
  \\
  \violet{\chV_\text{nn}}
  &= \sum_{A<B}^{M}\frac{Z_AZ_B}{R_{AB}}.
\end{align}
\label{eq:hamiltonian-terms}
\end{subequations}
The symbol $\laplacian$ denotes the Laplace operator, $M_A$ and $Z_A$ are
the mass and charge of nucleus $A$, $r_{iA}$ is the distance between
electron $i$ and nucleus $A$, $r_{ij}$ is the distance between electrons
$i$ and $j$, and $R_{AB}$ is the distance between nuclei $A$ and $B$.

Because nuclear masses are much larger than the electron mass,
$M_A\gg 1$ in atomic units, the \red{Born--Oppenheimer approximation}
decouples electronic and nuclear motion. The electrons then move in the
field of fixed nuclei, and the molecular wavefunction is written as
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\{\br_i\},\{\bR_A\})
  = \Phi_\text{nucl}(\{\bR_A\})
    \Phi_\text{elec}(\{\br_i\},\{\bR_A\}).
\end{equation}
The corresponding total energy at fixed nuclear geometry is
\begin{equation}
  \cE_\text{tot}
  = \cE_\text{elec}
  + \sum_{A<B}^{M}\frac{Z_AZ_B}{R_{AB}}.
\end{equation}
At a fixed nuclear geometry, the electronic factor satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \chH_\text{elec}\Phi_\text{elec}
  = \cE_\text{elec}\Phi_\text{elec},
  \qquad
  \boxed{
  \chH_\text{elec}
  = \blue{\chT_\text{e}}
  + \orange{\chV_\text{ne}}
  + \red{\chV_\text{ee}}
  }.
  \label{eq:electronic-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
The nuclear factor obeys
\begin{equation}
  \chH_\text{nucl}\Phi_\text{nucl}
  = \cE_\text{nucl}\Phi_\text{nucl},
  \qquad
  \boxed{
  \chH_\text{nucl}
  = \green{\chT_\text{n}}
  + \violet{\chV_\text{nn}}
  + \cE_\text{elec}(\{\bR_A\})
  }.
\end{equation}
Thus the nuclei move on the \red{potential-energy surface} defined by
$\violet{\chV_\text{nn}}+\cE_\text{elec}(\{\bR_A\})$.

\subsubsection{The Pauli principle}

Electrons are spin-$1/2$ fermions. Their spin functions satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\omega}=\ket{s,m_s},
  \qquad
  s^2\ket{s,m_s}=s(s+1)\ket{s,m_s},
  \qquad
  s_z\ket{s,m_s}=m_s\ket{s,m_s}.
\end{equation}
The two one-electron spin functions are
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\blue{\alpha}}
  = \ket{\tfrac{1}{2},+\tfrac{1}{2}},
  \qquad
  \ket{\red{\beta}}
  = \ket{\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2}},
\end{equation}
corresponding respectively to spin-up and spin-down electrons. They are
orthonormal,
\begin{align}
  \int\blue{\alpha}^*(\omega)\red{\beta}(\omega)\dd{\omega}
  &=\int\red{\beta}^*(\omega)\blue{\alpha}(\omega)\dd{\omega}=0,
  &
  \int\blue{\alpha}^*(\omega)\blue{\alpha}(\omega)\dd{\omega}
  &=\int\red{\beta}^*(\omega)\red{\beta}(\omega)\dd{\omega}=1,
  \\
  \braket{\blue{\alpha}}{\red{\beta}}
  &= \braket{\red{\beta}}{\blue{\alpha}}=0,
  &
  \braket{\blue{\alpha}}{\blue{\alpha}}
  &= \braket{\red{\beta}}{\red{\beta}}=1.
\end{align}
We combine the spin coordinate $\omega$ and spatial coordinate $\br$ into
the composite coordinate
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\violet{\bx}=(\orange{\omega},\red{\br})}.
\end{equation}

Indistinguishability requires the probability density to remain unchanged
when two particle labels are exchanged. For two particles,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \abs{\Psi(\red{\bx_1},\blue{\bx_2})}^2
  =\abs{\Psi(\blue{\bx_2},\red{\bx_1})}^2
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
  \Psi(\blue{\bx_2},\red{\bx_1})
  =\green{e^{\ii\theta}}\Psi(\red{\bx_1},\blue{\bx_2}) }.
\end{equation}
Applying the permutation operator $\chP_{12}$ twice restores the original
ordering:
\begin{align}
  \chP_{12}\Psi(\red{\bx_1},\blue{\bx_2})
  &=\Psi(\blue{\bx_2},\red{\bx_1})
  =\green{e^{\ii\theta}}\Psi(\red{\bx_1},\blue{\bx_2}),
  \\
  \chP_{12}^2\Psi(\red{\bx_1},\blue{\bx_2})
  &=\green{e^{2\ii\theta}}\Psi(\red{\bx_1},\blue{\bx_2})
  =\Psi(\red{\bx_1},\blue{\bx_2}).
\end{align}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
  \green{e^{2\ii\theta}}=1
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
  \green{\theta}=n\pi
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
  \boxed{\green{e^{\ii\theta}}=\pm1}.
\end{equation}
The plus sign defines \violet{bosons}, for which
$\violet{\Psi(\bx_1,\bx_2)=\Psi(\bx_2,\bx_1)}$. The minus sign defines
\orange{fermions}, for which
$\orange{\Psi(\bx_1,\bx_2)=-\Psi(\bx_2,\bx_1)}$.

The electronic Schr\"odinger equation and Pauli antisymmetry condition are
therefore
\begin{equation}
  \chH_\text{elec}\Phi(\bx_1,\bx_2,\ldots,\bx_N)
  =\cE_\text{elec}\Phi(\bx_1,\bx_2,\ldots,\bx_N),
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\bx_1,\ldots,\bx_i,\ldots,\bx_j,\ldots,\bx_N)
  =-
  \Phi(\bx_1,\ldots,\bx_j,\ldots,\bx_i,\ldots,\bx_N).
  \label{eq:pauli-antisymmetry}
\end{equation}
Putting the two particles at the same point, $\bx=\bx_1=\bx_2$, gives
\begin{equation}
  \text{For fermions,}\qquad
  \Psi(\bx,\bx)=-\Psi(\bx,\bx)
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
  \boxed{\Psi(\bx,\bx)=0}.
\end{equation}
The vanishing probability of finding two same-spin electrons at the same
point is the origin of the Fermi, or exchange, hole.

\paragraph{Problem.}
\violet{\textit{Given two one-electron functions $\chi_1(\bx)$ and
$\chi_2(\bx)$, construct a two-electron fermionic wavefunction
$\Psi(\bx_1,\bx_2)$.}}

\paragraph{Solution.}
A possible normalized antisymmetric combination is
\begin{equation}
  \Psi(\red{\bx_1},\blue{\bx_2})
  =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[
    \chi_1(\red{\bx_1})\chi_2(\blue{\bx_2})
    -\chi_2(\red{\bx_1})\chi_1(\blue{\bx_2})
  \right].
\end{equation}
Slater popularized the determinant representation of this combination,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \Psi(\red{\bx_1},\blue{\bx_2})
  =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
    \begin{vmatrix}
      \chi_1(\red{\bx_1})&\chi_2(\red{\bx_1})\\
      \chi_1(\blue{\bx_2})&\chi_2(\blue{\bx_2})
    \end{vmatrix}
  =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[
    \chi_1(\red{\bx_1})\chi_2(\blue{\bx_2})
    -\chi_2(\red{\bx_1})\chi_1(\blue{\bx_2})
  \right] }.
\end{equation}
This construction is a \red{Slater determinant}. By contrast, the simple
product
\begin{equation}
  \Psi(\bx_1,\bx_2)=\chi_1(\bx_1)\chi_2(\bx_2)
\end{equation}
is a \green{Hartree product} and does not possess the required antisymmetry.

\subsubsection{The Hartree--Fock wavefunction}

Hartree--Fock theory approximates the $N$-electron wavefunction by a single
Slater determinant,
\begin{align}
  \Psi_\text{HF}(\bx_1,\ldots,\bx_N)
  &=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}
  \begin{vmatrix}
    \chi_1(\bx_1)&\chi_2(\bx_1)&\cdots&\chi_N(\bx_1)\\
    \chi_1(\bx_2)&\chi_2(\bx_2)&\cdots&\chi_N(\bx_2)\\
    \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
    \chi_1(\bx_N)&\chi_2(\bx_N)&\cdots&\chi_N(\bx_N)
  \end{vmatrix}
  \\
  &\equiv \ket{\chi_1(\bx_1)\chi_2(\bx_2)\cdots\chi_N(\bx_N)}
  \\
  &=\blue{\chA}\left[
    \chi_1(\bx_1)\chi_2(\bx_2)\cdots\chi_N(\bx_N)
  \right]
  =\blue{\chA}\green{\Pi(\bx_1,\bx_2,\ldots,\bx_N)}.
  \label{eq:hf-determinant}
\end{align}
The operator $\blue{\chA}$ is the antisymmetrizer and
$\green{\Pi(\bx_1,\bx_2,\ldots,\bx_N)}$ is the Hartree product. Thus the
many-electron HF wavefunction is an antisymmetrized product of one-electron
functions.

Each spin orbital separates into spin and spatial factors,
\begin{equation}
  \chi_p(\bx)
  =\red{\sigma(\omega)}\blue{\psi_p^\sigma(\br)},
  \qquad
  \sigma(\omega)\in\{\alpha(\omega),\beta(\omega)\},
\end{equation}
and its spatial part is expanded as a linear combination of $K$ basis
functions,
\begin{equation}
  \blue{\psi_p^\sigma(\br)}
  =\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}C_{\mu p}^{\sigma}\green{\phi_\mu(\br)}.
  \label{eq:lcao-introduction}
\end{equation}
The spin orbitals are orthonormal,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \braket{\chi_p}{\chi_q}
  &=\int\chi_p^*(\bx)\chi_q(\bx)\dd{\bx}
  \\
  &=\delta_{pq}
  =\begin{cases}1&\text{if $p=q$},\\0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{pq}$ is the Kronecker delta. The underlying basis functions
are, a priori, not orthogonal. Their overlaps define the matrix elements
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \braket{\phi_\mu}{\phi_\nu}
  &=\int\phi_\mu^*(\br)\phi_\nu(\br)\dd{\br}
  \\
  &=S_{\mu\nu}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}

In restricted Hartree--Fock theory, which describes closed-shell systems,
the $\alpha$ and $\beta$ electrons in a pair share the same spatial orbital,
\begin{equation}
  \chi_p^\text{RHF}(\bx)
  =
  \begin{cases}
    \blue{\alpha(\omega)}\psi_p(\br),\\
    \red{\beta(\omega)}\psi_p(\br).
  \end{cases}
  =
  \begin{cases}
    \blue{\psi_p(\br)},\\
    \red{\Bar{\psi}_p(\br)}.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The closed-shell RHF ansatz does not describe open-shell systems. In
unrestricted Hartree--Fock theory, the two spin components may instead use
different spatial orbitals,
\begin{equation}
  \chi_p^\text{UHF}(\bx)
  =
  \begin{cases}
    \alpha(\omega)\blue{\psi_p^\alpha(\br)},\\
    \beta(\omega)\red{\psi_p^\beta(\br)}.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This additional freedom permits the treatment of open-shell systems, but it
may break spin symmetry. Restricted open-shell Hartree--Fock theory retains
shared spatial orbitals, but the slides do not discuss that formalism.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{RHF_UHF}
  \caption{Restricted and unrestricted choices for the spatial parts of the
  spin orbitals.}
  \label{fig:rhf-uhf-orbitals}
\end{figure}

Restricted spatial orbitals satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \braket{\psi_p}{\psi_q}
  =\int\psi_p^*(\br)\psi_q(\br)\dd{\br}=\delta_{pq}.
\end{equation}
For UHF orbitals, orthogonality holds separately within each spin sector,
\begin{align}
  \braket{\psi_p^\alpha}{\psi_q^\alpha}
  &=\int\psi_p^{\alpha*}(\br)\psi_q^\alpha(\br)\dd{\br}=\delta_{pq},
  \\
  \braket{\psi_p^\beta}{\psi_q^\beta}
  &=\int\psi_p^{\beta*}(\br)\psi_q^\beta(\br)\dd{\br}=\delta_{pq},
\end{align}
whereas opposite-spin spatial orbitals need not be orthogonal,
\begin{equation}
  \braket{\psi_p^\alpha}{\psi_q^\beta}
  =\int\psi_p^{\alpha*}(\br)\psi_q^\beta(\br)\dd{\br}
  =S_{pq}^{\alpha\beta}.
\end{equation}

The orbital sets and index conventions are
\begin{align}
  \{\phi_\mu\mid\mu=1,\ldots,K\}
  &:\ \green{\text{basis functions or atomic orbitals (AOs)}},
  \\
  \{\psi_p\mid p=1,\ldots,K\}
  &:\ \violet{\text{restricted spatial orbitals or molecular orbitals (MOs)}},
  \\
  \{\chi_p\mid p=1,\ldots,2K\}
  &:\ \orange{\text{spin orbitals}}.
\end{align}
With $K$ basis functions, one obtains $K$ spatial orbitals and $2K$ spin
orbitals, labeled by $\red{p,q,r,s,\ldots}$. Among the spin orbitals, $N$ are
occupied and carry indices $\red{i,j,k,l,\ldots}$. The remaining $2K-N$ are
unoccupied or vacant and carry indices $\red{a,b,c,d,\ldots}$.

A system with \red{two electrons in each occupied spatial orbital} is closed
shell. Otherwise it is open shell. For the ground state of a closed-shell
system, the first $N/2$ spatial orbitals are \red{doubly occupied}, and the
remaining $K-N/2$ spatial orbitals are \red{vacant}.

\subsection{The Hartree--Fock approximation}
\subsubsection{The Hartree--Fock energy}

For fixed nuclei, the Hartree--Fock energy is the expectation value of the
electronic Hamiltonian over the normalized Slater determinant,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\EHF=\mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\chH_\text{elec}}{\Psi_\text{HF}}},
  \qquad
  \chH_\text{elec}=\blue{\chT_\text{e}}+\orange{\chV_\text{ne}}
  +\red{\chV_\text{ee}}.
\end{equation}
It is useful to separate this Hamiltonian into one- and two-electron parts,
\begin{equation}
  \chH_\text{elec}=\chO_1+\chO_2
  =\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hh(\bx_i)+\sum_{i<j}^{N}\frac{1}{r_{ij}},
\end{equation}
where the one-electron, or core, Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
  \hh(\bx_i)=-\frac{\laplacian_i}{2}
  -\sum_{A=1}^{M}\frac{Z_A}{r_{iA}},
  \qquad
  \chO_1=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hh(\bx_i),
\end{equation}
and the electron--electron repulsion is
\begin{equation}
  \chO_2=\red{\chV_\text{ee}}=\sum_{i<j}^{N}\frac{1}{r_{ij}}.
\end{equation}

The Slater--Condon rules reduce the one-electron expectation value to a sum
over occupied spin orbitals,
\begin{equation}
  \mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\chO_1}{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  =\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mel{\chi_i}{\hh}{\chi_i}
  =\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{ii}.
\end{equation}
The nuclear repulsion does not depend on the electronic coordinates. For a
normalized electronic wavefunction,
\begin{equation}
  \mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\violet{\chV_\text{nn}}}{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  =\violet{V_\text{nn}}\braket{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  =\violet{V_\text{nn}}
  \qquad\text{if}\qquad
  \braket{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\Psi_\text{HF}}=1.
\end{equation}

The two-electron expectation value contains a \green{Coulomb} contribution
and a \red{exchange} contribution for each occupied pair,
\begin{align}
  \mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\chO_2}{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  &=\sum_{i<j}^{N}\left[
    \mel{\chi_i(\bx_1)\chi_j(\bx_2)}{r_{12}^{-1}}
        {\chi_i(\bx_1)\chi_j(\bx_2)}
  \right.\nonumber\\
  &\hspace{7em}\left.
    -\mel{\chi_i(\bx_1)\chi_j(\bx_2)}{r_{12}^{-1}}
        {\chi_j(\bx_1)\chi_i(\bx_2)}
  \right]
  \\
  &=\sum_{i<j}^{N}
    \left(\underbrace{\cJ_{ij}}_{\green{\text{Coulomb}}}
    -\underbrace{\cK_{ij}}_{\red{\text{exchange}}}\right)
  \\
  &=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}
    (\cJ_{ij}-\cK_{ij}).
\end{align}
The second form follows because $\red{\boxed{\cJ_{ii}=\cK_{ii}}}$, so the apparent
self-interaction cancels exactly. Combining the one- and two-electron terms
gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \EHF=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{ii}
  +\sum_{i<j}^{N}(\cJ_{ij}-\cK_{ij})
  }.
  \label{eq:hf-energy-spin-orbitals}
\end{equation}

The Coulomb operator generated by occupied spin orbital $\chi_j$ acts on
$\chi_i$ as
\begin{equation}
  \chJ_j(\bx_1)\ket{\chi_i(\bx_1)}
  =\mel{\chi_j(\bx_2)}{r_{12}^{-1}}{\chi_j(\bx_2)}
   \ket{\chi_i(\bx_1)}
  =\left[
    \int\chi_j^*(\bx_2)\frac{1}{r_{12}}\chi_j(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_2}
  \right]\ket{\chi_i(\bx_1)}.
\end{equation}
Its expectation value is
\begin{align}
  \cJ_{ij}
  &=\mel{\chi_i(\bx_1)}{\chJ_j(\bx_1)}{\chi_i(\bx_1)}
  \\
  &=\mel{\chi_i(\bx_1)\chi_j(\bx_2)}{r_{12}^{-1}}
    {\chi_i(\bx_1)\chi_j(\bx_2)}
  \\
  &=\iint \chi_i^*(\bx_1)\chi_j^*(\bx_2)\frac{1}{r_{12}}
    \chi_i(\bx_1)\chi_j(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_1}\dd{\bx_2}.
\end{align}
The Coulomb operator is local: after integration over $\bx_2$, it multiplies
$\chi_i(\bx_1)$ by a function of $\bx_1$.

The exchange operator instead acts according to
\begin{equation}
  \chK_j(\bx_1)\ket{\chi_i(\bx_1)}
  =\mel{\chi_j(\bx_2)}{r_{12}^{-1}}{\chi_i(\bx_2)}
   \ket{\chi_j(\bx_1)}
  =\left[
    \int\chi_j^*(\bx_2)\frac{1}{r_{12}}\chi_i(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_2}
  \right]\ket{\chi_j(\bx_1)}.
\end{equation}
It is therefore nonlocal. Its matrix element is
\begin{align}
  \cK_{ij}
  &=\mel{\chi_i(\bx_1)}{\chK_j(\bx_1)}{\chi_i(\bx_1)}
  \\
  &=\mel{\chi_i(\bx_1)\chi_j(\bx_2)}{r_{12}^{-1}}
    {\chi_j(\bx_1)\chi_i(\bx_2)}
  \\
  &=\iint \chi_i^*(\bx_1)\chi_j^*(\bx_2)\frac{1}{r_{12}}
    \chi_j(\bx_1)\chi_i(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_1}\dd{\bx_2}.
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Integrals}

We use both physicists' and chemists' notations. A one-electron integral is
written equivalently as
\begin{equation}
  \mel{i}{\hh}{j}=h_{ij}=\mel{\chi_i}{\hh}{\chi_j}
  =\int\chi_i^*(\bx_1)\hh(\bx_1)\chi_j(\bx_1)\dd{\bx_1}.
\end{equation}
In physicists' notation, an electron-repulsion integral (ERI) is
\begin{equation}
  \braket{ij}{kl}
  =\braket{\chi_i\chi_j}{\chi_k\chi_l}
  =\iint\chi_i^*(\bx_1)\chi_j^*(\bx_2)\frac{1}{r_{12}}
  \chi_k(\bx_1)\chi_l(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_1}\dd{\bx_2},
\end{equation}
and the corresponding antisymmetrized integral is
\begin{equation}
  \mel{ij}{}{kl}
  =\braket{ij}{kl}-\braket{ij}{lk}
  =\iint\chi_i^*(\bx_1)\chi_j^*(\bx_2)\frac{1}{r_{12}}
   (1-\chP_{12})\chi_k(\bx_1)\chi_l(\bx_2)
   \dd{\bx_1}\dd{\bx_2}.
\end{equation}
In Mulliken, or chemists', notation, the same one-electron integral is
\begin{equation}
  \rmel{i}{\hh}{j}=h_{ij}=\rmel{\chi_i}{\hh}{\chi_j}
  =\int\chi_i^*(\bx_1)\hh(\bx_1)\chi_j(\bx_1)\dd{\bx_1},
\end{equation}
while the ERI is
\begin{equation}
  \rbraket{ij}{kl}
  =\rbraket{\chi_i\chi_j}{\chi_k\chi_l}
  =\iint\chi_i^*(\bx_1)\chi_j(\bx_1)\frac{1}{r_{12}}
  \chi_k^*(\bx_2)\chi_l(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_1}\dd{\bx_2}.
\end{equation}
The same notation will be used for integrals over spin orbitals and spatial
orbitals; the surrounding context specifies which type is intended.

For reference, the physicists' ERI is again
\begin{equation}
  \braket{ij}{kl}
  =\braket{\chi_i\chi_j}{\chi_k\chi_l}
  =\iint\chi_i^*(\bx_1)\chi_j^*(\bx_2)\frac{1}{r_{12}}
  \chi_k(\bx_1)\chi_l(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_1}\dd{\bx_2}.
\end{equation}
For \red{complex-valued integrals}, physicists' ERIs obey
\begin{equation}
  \braket{ij}{kl}=\braket{ji}{lk}
  =\braket{kl}{ij}^*=\braket{lk}{ji}^*.
\end{equation}
For \green{real-valued integrals}, this becomes
\begin{align}
  \braket{ij}{kl}
  &=\braket{ji}{lk}=\braket{kl}{ij}=\braket{lk}{ji}
  \\
  &=\braket{kj}{il}=\braket{jk}{li}=\braket{il}{kj}
  =\braket{li}{jk}.
\end{align}
In chemists' notation, the defining integral is
\begin{equation}
  \rbraket{ij}{kl}
  =\rbraket{\chi_i\chi_j}{\chi_k\chi_l}
  =\iint\chi_i^*(\bx_1)\chi_j(\bx_1)\frac{1}{r_{12}}
  \chi_k^*(\bx_2)\chi_l(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_1}\dd{\bx_2},
\end{equation}
and the \green{real-valued} symmetry is
\begin{align}
  \rbraket{ij}{kl}
  &=\rbraket{ji}{kl}=\rbraket{ij}{lk}=\rbraket{ji}{lk}
  \\
  &=\rbraket{kl}{ij}=\rbraket{lk}{ij}=\rbraket{kl}{ji}
  =\rbraket{lk}{ji}.
\end{align}
Thus, for real-valued orbitals, ERIs possess
\red{eightfold permutation symmetry}.

\subsubsection{Examples}

\paragraph{Problem: normalization of the HF wavefunction.}
\violet{\textit{Show that the HF wavefunction built with two normalized,
orthogonal spin orbitals $\chi_1$ and $\chi_2$ is normalized.}}

\paragraph{Solution.}
The determinant and its product expansion are
\begin{equation}
  \Psi_\text{HF}
  =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
   \begin{vmatrix}\chi_1(1)&\chi_2(1)\\\chi_1(2)&\chi_2(2)\end{vmatrix}
  =\frac{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)-\chi_1(2)\chi_2(1)}{\sqrt{2}}.
\end{equation}
Its norm is
\begin{align}
  \braket{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  &=\frac{1}{2}
    \braket{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)-\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}
            {\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)-\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}
  \\
  &=\frac{1}{2}\Big[
    \braket{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)}{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)}
    -\braket{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)}{\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}
  \nonumber\\
  &\hspace{5em}
    -\braket{\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)}
    +\braket{\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}{\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}\Big]
  \\
  &=\frac{1}{2}[1-0-0+1]=1.
\end{align}
In the first diagonal term, for example, separability gives
\begin{equation}
  \braket{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)}{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)}
  =\braket{\chi_1(1)}{\chi_1(1)}
   \braket{\chi_2(2)}{\chi_2(2)}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Problem: core Hamiltonian.}
\violet{\textit{Show for the same system that
$\mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\chO_1}{\Psi_\text{HF}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{ii}$.}}

\paragraph{Solution.}
For two electrons,
\begin{equation}
  \chO_1=\hh(1)+\hh(2).
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{align}
  &\mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\hh(1)+\hh(2)}{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{1}{2}
  \mel{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)-\chi_1(2)\chi_2(1)}
      {\hh(1)+\hh(2)}
      {\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)-\chi_1(2)\chi_2(1)}
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{1}{2}\Big[
  \mel{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)}{\hh(1)+\hh(2)}{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)}
  \nonumber\\
  &\qquad-\mel{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)}{\hh(1)+\hh(2)}{\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}
  \nonumber\\
  &\qquad-\mel{\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}{\hh(1)+\hh(2)}{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)}
  \nonumber\\
  &\qquad+\mel{\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}{\hh(1)+\hh(2)}{\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}
  \Big]
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{1}{2}[h_{11}+h_{22}-0-0+h_{22}+h_{11}]
  =h_{11}+h_{22}.
\end{align}

\paragraph{Problem: two-electron Hamiltonian.}
\violet{\textit{Show for the same system that}}
\begin{equation}
  \mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\chO_2}{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  =\sum_{i<j}^{N}(\cJ_{ij}-\cK_{ij}),
\end{equation}
\violet{\textit{and write down the HF energy.}}

\paragraph{Solution.}
Here $\chO_2=r_{12}^{-1}$, and direct expansion gives
\begin{align}
  \mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{r_{12}^{-1}}{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  &=\frac{1}{2}
    \mel{\chi_1\chi_2-\chi_2\chi_1}{r_{12}^{-1}}
        {\chi_1\chi_2-\chi_2\chi_1}
  \\
  &=\frac{1}{2}\Big[
    \mel{\chi_1\chi_2}{r_{12}^{-1}}{\chi_1\chi_2}
    -\mel{\chi_1\chi_2}{r_{12}^{-1}}{\chi_2\chi_1}
  \nonumber\\
  &\hspace{5em}
    -\mel{\chi_2\chi_1}{r_{12}^{-1}}{\chi_1\chi_2}
    +\mel{\chi_2\chi_1}{r_{12}^{-1}}{\chi_2\chi_1}\Big]
  \\
  &=\frac{1}{2}[\cJ_{12}-\cK_{12}-\cK_{12}+\cJ_{12}]
  =\cJ_{12}-\cK_{12}.
\end{align}
The last equality uses
\begin{equation}
  \mel{\chi_2\chi_1}{r_{12}^{-1}}{\chi_2\chi_1}
  =\mel{\chi_1\chi_2}{r_{12}^{-1}}{\chi_1\chi_2}.
\end{equation}
The complete energy is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{E_\text{HF}=h_{11}+h_{22}+\cJ_{12}-\cK_{12}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Problem: three-electron system.}
\violet{\textit{Find the HF energy of a three-electron system composed of
the spin orbitals $\chi_1$, $\chi_2$, and $\chi_3$.}}

\paragraph{Solution.}
The one- and two-electron operators are
\begin{gather}
  \chO_1=\hh(\bx_1)+\hh(\bx_2)+\hh(\bx_3),
  \\
  \chO_2=r_{12}^{-1}+r_{13}^{-1}+r_{23}^{-1}.
\end{gather}
Applying the preceding expansion to all three electron pairs gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  E_\text{HF}=h_{11}+h_{22}+h_{33}
  +\cJ_{12}+\cJ_{13}+\cJ_{23}
  -\cK_{12}-\cK_{13}-\cK_{23} }.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{From spin orbitals to spatial orbitals}

Consider closed-shell \ce{H2} in a minimal basis. In the spin-orbital
representation, its energy is
\begin{equation}
  \EHF
  =\mel{\chi_1}{\hh}{\chi_1}+\mel{\chi_2}{\hh}{\chi_2}
  +\braket{\chi_1\chi_2}{\chi_1\chi_2}
  -\braket{\chi_1\chi_2}{\chi_2\chi_1}.
\end{equation}
The two occupied spin orbitals share the same spatial orbital. The
transformation from spin orbitals to spatial orbitals is
\begin{align}
  \chi_1(\bx)&\equiv\psi_1(\bx)=\psi_1(\br)\alpha(\omega),
  \\
  \chi_2(\bx)&\equiv\Bar{\psi}_1(\bx)=\psi_1(\br)\beta(\omega).
\end{align}
Consequently, the same energy may be written as
\begin{equation}
  \EHF
  =\rmel{\psi_1}{\hh}{\psi_1}
  +\rmel{\Bar{\psi}_1}{\hh}{\Bar{\psi}_1}
  +\rbraket{\psi_1\psi_1}{\Bar{\psi}_1\Bar{\psi}_1}
  -\rbraket{\psi_1\Bar{\psi}_1}{\Bar{\psi}_1\psi_1}.
\end{equation}

The core Hamiltonian acts only on the spatial coordinate. Consequently, the
spin and spatial integrations factorize. For the $\alpha$ spin orbital,
\begin{align}
  \rmel{\chi_1}{\hh}{\chi_1}
  &=\int\chi_1^*(\bx)\hh(\bx)\chi_1(\bx)\dd{\bx}
  \\
  &=\iint\alpha^*(\omega)\psi_1^*(\br)\hh(\br)
    \alpha(\omega)\psi_1(\br)\dd{\omega}\dd{\br}
  \\
  &=\underbrace{\qty[\int\alpha^*(\omega)\alpha(\omega)\dd{\omega}]}_{=1}
    \underbrace{\qty[\int\psi_1^*(\br)\hh(\br)\psi_1(\br)\dd{\br}]}
    _{\rmel{\psi_1}{\hh}{\psi_1}}.
\end{align}
For the $\beta$ spin orbital, the corresponding steps are
\begin{align}
  \rmel{\chi_2}{\hh}{\chi_2}
  &=\int\chi_2^*(\bx)\hh(\bx)\chi_2(\bx)\dd{\bx}
  \\
  &=\iint\beta^*(\omega)\psi_1^*(\br)\hh(\br)
    \beta(\omega)\psi_1(\br)\dd{\omega}\dd{\br}
  \\
  &=\underbrace{\qty[\int\beta^*(\omega)\beta(\omega)\dd{\omega}]}_{=1}
    \underbrace{\qty[\int\psi_1^*(\br)\hh(\br)\psi_1(\br)\dd{\br}]}
    _{\rmel{\psi_1}{\hh}{\psi_1}}.
\end{align}
Thus, both electrons contribute the same spatial one-electron integral.

The Coulomb term contains diagonal spin overlaps and therefore survives the
spin integration. The complete integration is
\begin{align}
  \rbraket{\chi_1\chi_1}{\chi_2\chi_2}
  &=\iint\chi_1^*(\bx_1)\chi_1(\bx_1)r_{12}^{-1}
    \chi_2^*(\bx_2)\chi_2(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_1}\dd{\bx_2}
  \\
  &=\iiiint\alpha^*(\omega_1)\psi_1^*(\br_1)
    \alpha(\omega_1)\psi_1(\br_1)r_{12}^{-1}
    \notag\\
  &\qquad\times\beta^*(\omega_2)\psi_1^*(\br_2)
    \beta(\omega_2)\psi_1(\br_2)
    \dd{\omega_1}\dd{\br_1}\dd{\omega_2}\dd{\br_2}
  \\
  &=\underbrace{\qty[\int\alpha^*(\omega_1)\alpha(\omega_1)
      \dd{\omega_1}]}_{=1}
    \underbrace{\qty[\int\beta^*(\omega_2)\beta(\omega_2)
      \dd{\omega_2}]}_{=1}
    \notag\\
  &\qquad\times
    \underbrace{\qty[\iint\psi_1^*(\br_1)\psi_1(\br_1)r_{12}^{-1}
      \psi_1^*(\br_2)\psi_1(\br_2)\dd{\br_1}\dd{\br_2}]}
      _{\rbraket{\psi_1\psi_1}{\psi_1\psi_1}}.
\end{align}
The exchange term contains the opposite-spin overlaps
$\braket{\alpha}{\beta}$ and $\braket{\beta}{\alpha}$, both of which vanish.
The complete exchange integration is
\begin{align}
  \rbraket{\chi_1\chi_2}{\chi_2\chi_1}
  &=\iint\chi_1^*(\bx_1)\chi_2(\bx_1)r_{12}^{-1}
    \chi_2^*(\bx_2)\chi_1(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_1}\dd{\bx_2}
  \\
  &=\iiiint\alpha^*(\omega_1)\psi_1^*(\br_1)
    \beta(\omega_1)\psi_1(\br_1)r_{12}^{-1}
    \notag\\
  &\qquad\times\beta^*(\omega_2)\psi_1^*(\br_2)
    \alpha(\omega_2)\psi_1(\br_2)
    \dd{\omega_1}\dd{\br_1}\dd{\omega_2}\dd{\br_2}
  \\
  &=\underbrace{\qty[\int\alpha^*(\omega_1)\beta(\omega_1)
      \dd{\omega_1}]}_{=0}
    \underbrace{\qty[\int\beta^*(\omega_2)\alpha(\omega_2)
      \dd{\omega_2}]}_{=0}
    \notag\\
  &\qquad\times
    \underbrace{\qty[\iint\psi_1^*(\br_1)\psi_1(\br_1)r_{12}^{-1}
      \psi_1^*(\br_2)\psi_1(\br_2)\dd{\br_1}\dd{\br_2}]}
      _{\rbraket{\psi_1\psi_1}{\psi_1\psi_1}}
  =0.
\end{align}
Hence the spatial-orbital expression for minimal-basis \ce{H2} is
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \EHF=2\rmel{\psi_1}{\hh}{\psi_1}
  +\rbraket{\psi_1\psi_1}{\psi_1\psi_1}
  =2\rmel{1}{\hh}{1}+\rbraket{11}{11}
  }.
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{H2}
  \caption{Closed-shell occupation of the spatial orbital in the minimal-basis
  description of \ce{H2}.}
  \label{fig:h2-closed-shell}
\end{figure}

For a general closed-shell determinant, every occupied spatial orbital has
one $\alpha$ and one $\beta$ electron. The general expression in spin and
spatial orbitals is
\begin{align}
  \EHF
  &=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mel{i}{\hh}{i}
    +\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}
    \qty[\braket{ij}{ij}-\braket{ij}{ji}]
  \\
  &=2\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}\rmel{i}{\hh}{i}
    +\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N/2}
    \qty[2\rbraket{ii}{jj}-\rbraket{ij}{ji}].
\end{align}
The one-electron contribution becomes
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i=1}^{N}\mel{i}{\hh}{i}
  =\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}\mel{i}{\hh}{i}
  +\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}\mel{\Bar{i}}{\hh}{\Bar{i}}
  =2\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}\rmel{i}{\hh}{i}.
\end{equation}
Separating all four spin combinations in the two-electron contribution gives
\begin{align}
  &\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}
    \qty[\braket{ij}{ij}-\braket{ij}{ji}]
  \notag\\
  &\quad=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg\{
    \sum_{i=1}^{N/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N/2}
      \qty(\braket{ij}{ij}-\braket{ij}{ji})
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\quad+
    \sum_{i=1}^{N/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N/2}
      \qty(\braket{\Bar{i}j}{\Bar{i}j}-\braket{\Bar{i}j}{j\Bar{i}})
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\quad+
    \sum_{i=1}^{N/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N/2}
      \qty(\braket{i\Bar{j}}{i\Bar{j}}-\braket{i\Bar{j}}{\Bar{j}i})
  \notag\\
  &\qquad\quad+
    \sum_{i=1}^{N/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N/2}
      \qty(\braket{\Bar{i}\Bar{j}}{\Bar{i}\Bar{j}}
      -\braket{\Bar{i}\Bar{j}}{\Bar{j}\Bar{i}})
    \Bigg\}
  \\
  &\quad=\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N/2}
    \qty[2\rbraket{ii}{jj}-\rbraket{ij}{ji}].
\end{align}
Combining the one- and two-electron contributions gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \EHF
  =2\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}\rmel{i}{\hh}{i}
  +\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N/2}
  \left[2\rbraket{ii}{jj}-\rbraket{ij}{ji}\right]
  }.
  \label{eq:rhf-energy-spatial}
\end{equation}
The factor of two in the one-electron part comes from the two spin functions
associated with each occupied spatial orbital. In the two-electron part, all
four spin combinations contribute Coulomb terms, whereas exchange survives
only between electrons of the same spin.

\paragraph{Problem: HF energy of the \ce{Li} atom.}

\violet{``Find the HF energy of the \ce{Li} atom in terms of spatial
orbitals.''}

\paragraph{Solution.}

The three occupied spin orbitals are
\begin{equation}
  \chi_1=\alpha\psi_1,
  \qquad \chi_2=\beta\psi_1,
  \qquad \chi_3=\alpha\psi_2.
\end{equation}
The energy in terms of spatial-orbital integrals is then
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{E_\text{HF}=2h_{11}+h_{22}+J_{11}+2J_{12}-K_{12}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Problem: HF energy of the \ce{B} atom.}

\violet{``Find the HF energy of the \ce{B} atom in terms of spatial
orbitals.''}

\paragraph{Solution.}

For the occupation used for the \ce{B} atom, the corresponding expression is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  E_\text{HF}={}&2h_{11}+2h_{22}+h_{33}+J_{11}+4J_{12}+J_{22}-2K_{12}
  \\
  &+2J_{13}+2J_{23}-K_{13}-K_{23}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Excited determinants}

In the reference ground-state determinant,
\red{the electrons are in the $N$ lowest spin orbitals (Aufbau principle)}:
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\Psi_0}
  =\ket{\chi_1\ldots\chi_{\green{i}}\chi_{\green{j}}\ldots\chi_N}.
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.34\textwidth]{HF_det}
  \caption{Reference Hartree--Fock determinant constructed from the occupied
  spin orbitals.}

\end{figure}

For a singly excited determinant, the electron in $\green{i}$ is promoted to
$\red{a}$. In determinant and second-quantized notation,
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\Psi_{\green{i}}^{\red{a}}}
  =\ket*{\chi_1\ldots\chi_{\red{a}}\chi_{\green{j}}\ldots\chi_N}
  =\cre{\red{a}}\ani{\green{i}}\ket{\Psi_0}.
\end{equation}
For a doubly excited determinant, the electrons in $\green{i}$ and
$\green{j}$ are promoted to $\red{a}$ and $\red{b}$, respectively:
\begin{equation}
  \ket*{\Psi_{\green{ij}}^{\red{ab}}}
  =\ket{\chi_1\ldots\chi_{\red{a}}\chi_{\red{b}}\ldots\chi_N}
  =\cre{\red{a}}\cre{\red{b}}\ani{\green{j}}\ani{\green{i}}
   \ket{\Psi_0}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Slater--Condon rules}

The Slater--Condon rules express matrix elements between determinants in
terms of spin-orbital integrals. For the one-electron operator
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\chO_1=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hh(\br_i)},
\end{equation}
the result depends on the number of spin orbitals by which the determinants
differ.

\paragraph{Case 1: determinants differ by zero spin orbitals.}

If $\ket{\red{K}}=\ket{\cdots i j\cdots}$, the diagonal matrix element is
\begin{equation}
  \mel{\red{K}}{\chO_1}{\red{K}}
  =\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mel{i}{\hh}{i}.
\end{equation}
For example,
\begin{equation}
  \mel{\Psi_0}{\chO_1}{\Psi_0}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{ii}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Case 2: determinants differ by one spin orbital.}

Let $\ket{\red{K}}=\ket{\cdots i j\cdots}$ and
$\ket{\blue{L}}=\ket{\cdots k j\cdots}$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \mel{\red{K}}{\chO_1}{\blue{L}}=\mel{i}{\hh}{k}.
\end{equation}
For example,
\begin{equation}
  \mel*{\Psi_0}{\chO_1}{\Psi_{\green{i}}^{\red{a}}}
  =h_{\green{i}\red{a}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Case 3: determinants differ by two spin orbitals.}

Let $\ket{\red{K}}=\ket{\cdots i j\ldots}$ and
$\ket{\blue{L}}=\ket{\cdots k l\ldots}$. A one-electron operator cannot
connect these determinants, and therefore
\begin{equation}
  \mel{\red{K}}{\chO_1}{\blue{L}}=0.
\end{equation}
For example,
\begin{equation}
  \mel*{\Psi_0}{\chO_1}{\Psi_{\green{ij}}^{\red{ab}}}=0.
\end{equation}

For the two-electron operator
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\chO_2=\sum_{i<j}^{N}r_{ij}^{-1}},
\end{equation}
the corresponding three cases are as follows.

\paragraph{Case 1: determinants differ by zero spin orbitals.}

If $\ket{\red{K}}=\ket{\cdots i j\cdots}$, then
\begin{equation}
  \mel{\red{K}}{\chO_2}{\red{K}}
  =\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}^{N}\mel{ij}{}{ij}.
\end{equation}
For example,
\begin{equation}
  \mel*{\Psi_0}{\chO_2}{\Psi_0}
  =\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}^{N}\mel{ij}{}{ij}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Case 2: determinants differ by one spin orbital.}

Let $\ket{\red{K}}=\ket{\cdots i j\cdots}$ and
$\ket{\blue{L}}=\ket{\cdots k j\cdots}$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \mel{\red{K}}{\chO_2}{\blue{L}}=\sum_{j}^{N}\mel{ij}{}{kj}.
\end{equation}
For example,
\begin{equation}
  \mel*{\Psi_0}{\chO_2}{\Psi_{\green{i}}^{\red{a}}}
  =\sum_{k}^{N}\mel{\green{i}k}{}{\red{a}k}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Case 3: determinants differ by two spin orbitals.}

Let $\ket{\red{K}}=\ket{\cdots i j\cdots}$ and
$\ket{\blue{L}}=\ket{\cdots k l\cdots}$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \mel{\red{K}}{\chO_2}{\blue{L}}=\mel{ij}{}{kl}.
\end{equation}
For example,
\begin{equation}
  \mel*{\Psi_0}{\chO_2}{\Psi_{\green{ij}}^{\red{ab}}}
  =\mel{\green{ij}}{}{\red{ab}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Excited determinant versus excited HF solution.}

An excited determinant is
\begin{itemize}
  \item[\cmark] an orbital substitution within a \red{fixed} set of reference
    orbitals;
  \item[\cmark] a valid many-electron basis function;
  \item[\cmark] a building block for configuration-interaction and
    coupled-cluster expansions.
\end{itemize}
In general, an excited determinant is not
\begin{itemize}
  \item[\xmark] a separately optimized HF state;
  \item[\xmark] a stationary point of the HF energy;
  \item[\xmark] an eigenfunction of $\chS^2$.
\end{itemize}
Thus, \red{orbital excitation and state optimization are different
procedures}.

For a stationary HF reference, \red{Brillouin's theorem} states that the
Hamiltonian does not couple the reference determinant to any singly excited
determinant,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \mel{\Psi_0}{\chH_\text{elec}}{\Psi_{\green{i}}^{\red{a}}}
  =h_{\green{i}\red{a}}
  +\sum_{k}^{N}\mel{\green{i}k}{}{\red{a}k}
  =f_{\green{i}\red{a}}=0
  }.
  \label{eq:brillouin-theorem}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Spin quantum numbers.}
Within the nonrelativistic spin-free framework, the Hamiltonian commutes with
the total-spin operators,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \comm{\chH}{\purple{\chS^2}}=0,
  \qquad
  \comm{\chH}{\violet{\chS_z}}=0
  }.
\end{equation}
Its eigenfunctions may therefore be chosen as simultaneous eigenfunctions of
$\chS^2$ and $\chS_z$,
\begin{equation}
  \purple{\chS^2}\ket{S,M_S}=S(S+1)\ket{S,M_S},
  \qquad
  \violet{\chS_z}\ket{S,M_S}=M_S\ket{S,M_S}.
\end{equation}
The spin quantum number $S$ determines the \purple{spin multiplicity} $2S+1$: $S=0$,
$1/2$, and $1$ correspond to singlets, doublets, and triplets. The projection
quantum number takes the values $M_S=-S,-S+1,\ldots,S-1,S$ and is the
\violet{spin projection}.

For a collinear determinant containing $N^\alpha$ spin-up and $N^\beta$
spin-down orbitals,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \violet{\chS_z}\ket{\Psi}=M_S\ket{\Psi},
  \qquad
  M_S=\frac{N^\alpha-N^\beta}{2}
  }.
\end{equation}
Here, collinear means that all occupied spin orbitals use one common spin
quantization axis. Every RHF or UHF determinant of this form has a definite
$M_S$.

\paragraph{Closed-shell RHF.}

Every spatial orbital is doubly occupied, and therefore
\begin{equation}
  \purple{\chS^2}\ket{\Psi_\text{RHF}}=0.
\end{equation}
The determinant is a \red{pure singlet}.

\paragraph{Open-shell UHF.}

The spin-up and spin-down spatial orbitals are different and give, in
general,
\begin{equation}
  \purple{\chS^2}\ket{\Psi_\text{UHF}}
  \neq S(S+1)\ket{\Psi_\text{UHF}}.
\end{equation}
Thus, \red{every RHF or UHF determinant has a well-defined $M_S$, but only
specific determinants have a well-defined $S$}.

For $N^\alpha\geq N^\beta$, the UHF expectation value is
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \expval{\purple{\chS^2}}_\text{UHF}
  =M_S(M_S+1)+N^\beta
  -\sum_{i=1}^{N^\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{N^\beta}
   \abs{S_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}}^2
  },
  \qquad
  \underbrace{S_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}}_{\text{overlap}}
  =\braket{\psi_i^\alpha}{\psi_j^\beta}.
  \label{eq:uhf-spin-contamination}
\end{equation}
For a target high-spin state with $S=M_S$, the spin contamination is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta S^2
  =\expval{\purple{\chS^2}}_\text{UHF}
  -\expval{\purple{\chS^2}}_\text{exact}\geq0.
\end{equation}
More explicitly,
\begin{itemize}
  \item a singlet may be contaminated by triplets, quintets, \ldots;
  \item a doublet may be contaminated by quartets, sextets, \ldots;
  \item a triplet may be contaminated by quintets, septets, \ldots;
  \item restoring spin symmetry generally requires \red{spin projection} or
    \red{a linear combination of determinants}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Spin adaptation of singly excited determinants.}
Starting from a closed-shell singlet reference, define the two $M_S=0$
determinants
\begin{equation}
  \ket{D_\alpha}=\cre{a\alpha}\ani{i\alpha}\ket{\Psi_0},
  \qquad
  \ket{D_\beta}=\cre{a\beta}\ani{i\beta}\ket{\Psi_0}.
\end{equation}
Both determinants satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \violet{\chS_z}\ket{D_\sigma}=0,
  \qquad
  \mel{D_\sigma}{\purple{\chS^2}}{D_\sigma}=1.
\end{equation}

Each determinant has $M_S=0$, but contains equal singlet and triplet
character. Thus, neither determinant has a definite total spin: being an
eigenfunction of $\violet{\chS_z}$ does not imply being an eigenfunction of
$\purple{\chS^2}$. In particular, \red{open-shell determinants might not be
pure spin functions}.

In the basis $\{\ket{D_\alpha},\ket{D_\beta}\}$,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  [\purple{\chS^2}]_{\{D_\alpha,D_\beta\}}
  =\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}
  }.
\end{equation}
Diagonalizing this matrix produces the pure singlet
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \ket{{}^1\Psi_i^a}
  =\frac{\ket{D_\alpha}\red{+}\ket{D_\beta}}{\sqrt{2}}
  },
  \qquad
  \purple{\chS^2}\ket{{}^1\Psi_i^a}=0
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad S=0,
\end{equation}
and the $M_S=0$ component of the pure triplet,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \ket{{}^{3,0}\Psi_i^a}
  =\frac{\ket{D_\alpha}\red{-}\ket{D_\beta}}{\sqrt{2}}
  },
  \qquad
  \purple{\chS^2}\ket{{}^{3,0}\Psi_i^a}
  =2\ket{{}^{3,0}\Psi_i^a}
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad S=1.
\end{equation}
Thus, \red{spin adaptation is the diagonalization of
$\purple{\chS^2}$ in a determinant space}.

For two open-shell spatial orbitals $i$ and $a$, the singlet separates into a
spatially symmetric factor and a spin-antisymmetric factor,
\begin{equation}
  \ket{{}^1\Psi_i^a}
  =\frac{1}{2}
  \underbrace{\qty[
    \ket{\phi_i(1)\phi_a(2)}+\ket{\phi_a(1)\phi_i(2)}
  ]}_{\text{spatially symmetric}}
  \underbrace{\qty[
    \alpha(1)\beta(2)-\beta(1)\alpha(2)
  ]}_{\text{spin antisymmetric}}.
\end{equation}
This is a \green{symmetric spatial part} multiplied by an
\red{antisymmetric spin part}.
The $M_S=0$ triplet instead combines a spatially antisymmetric factor with a
spin-symmetric factor,
\begin{equation}
  \ket{{}^{3,0}\Psi_i^a}
  =\frac{1}{2}
  \underbrace{\qty[
    \ket{\phi_i(1)\phi_a(2)}-\ket{\phi_a(1)\phi_i(2)}
  ]}_{\text{spatially antisymmetric}}
  \underbrace{\qty[
    \alpha(1)\beta(2)+\beta(1)\alpha(2)
  ]}_{\text{spin symmetric}}.
\end{equation}
This is an \red{antisymmetric spatial part} multiplied by a
\green{symmetric spin part}. \red{The total electronic wavefunction is
antisymmetric in both cases}.

The three components of the $S=1$ multiplet are
\begin{align}
  \ket{{}^{3,+1}\Psi_i^a}&=\ket{\cdots i^\alpha a^\alpha\cdots},
  &
  \ket{{}^{3,0}\Psi_i^a}
  &=\frac{\ket{D_\alpha}-\ket{D_\beta}}{\sqrt{2}},
  &
  \ket{{}^{3,-1}\Psi_i^a}&=\ket{\cdots i^\beta a^\beta\cdots}.
\end{align}
Each satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \purple{\chS^2}\ket{1,M_S}=2\ket{1,M_S},
  \qquad
  \violet{\chS_z}\ket{1,M_S}=M_S\ket{1,M_S}.
\end{equation}
The ladder operators connect the components according to
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\chS_\pm}_{\text{\red{ladder operator}}}\ket{S,M_S}
  =\sqrt{S(S+1)-M_S(M_S\pm1)}\ket{S,M_S\pm1}.
\end{equation}
The $M_S=\pm1$ components are single spin-pure determinants, while the pure
$M_S=0$ triplet requires two determinants.

\subsubsection{The Fock operator}

The occupied spin orbitals minimize the HF energy subject to orthonormality.
Introducing the matrix of \red{Lagrange multipliers} $\epsilon_{ji}$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}[\{\chi_i\}]=E_\text{HF}[\{\chi_i\}]
  -\sum_{ij}\epsilon_{ji}
  \left(\braket{\chi_i}{\chi_j}-\delta_{ij}\right).
\end{equation}
The stationarity condition $\fdv*{\mathcal{L}}{\chi_i^*(\bx)}=0$ produces
the coupled HF equations
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\hf(\bx)\chi_i(\bx)=\sum_j\epsilon_{ji}\chi_j(\bx)},
\end{equation}
with the one-electron Fock operator
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \hf(\bx)=\hh(\bx)
  +\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N}[\chJ_i(\bx)-\chK_i(\bx)]}
  _{\Hat{v}^{\text{HF}}(\bx)} }.
  \label{eq:fock-operator}
\end{equation}
The second term is the \red{Hartree--Fock potential}.

The Hermitian multiplier matrix can be diagonalized by a unitary
transformation $\purple{\bU}$,
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde{\bepsilon}=\purple{\bU}^{\dag}\bepsilon\purple{\bU},
  \qquad
  \widetilde{\chi}_i=\sum_j\chi_j\purple{U}_{ji}.
\end{equation}
This occupied-orbital rotation leaves the HF state unchanged apart from a
phase. Indeed, $\purple{\bU}^{\dag}\purple{\bU}=\bI$ implies
$\det(\purple{\bU})=e^{\ii\theta}$ and
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\widetilde{\Psi}_\text{HF}}
  =e^{\ii\theta}\ket{\Psi_\text{HF}}.
\end{equation}
After dropping the tildes, one obtains the \red{canonical HF equations}
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\hf(\bx)\chi_i(\bx)=\epsilon_i\chi_i(\bx)}.
  \label{eq:canonical-hf}
\end{equation}
Their eigenfunctions are the canonical spin orbitals and their eigenvalues
are the orbital energies.

The Fock matrix elements in the spin-orbital basis are
\begin{align}
  f_{ij}=\mel{\chi_i}{\hf}{\chi_j}
  &=\mel{i}{\hh}{j}
    +\sum_k\left[\braket{ik}{jk}-\braket{ik}{kj}\right]
  \\
  &=\mel{i}{\hh}{j}+\sum_k\mel{ik}{}{jk}.
\end{align}
Projection of Eq.~\eqref{eq:canonical-hf} onto $\chi_i$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \epsilon_i=\mel{i}{\hh}{i}
  +\sum_k\left[\braket{ik}{ik}-\braket{ik}{ki}\right]
  =\mel{i}{\hh}{i}+\sum_k\mel{ik}{}{ik} }.
\end{equation}
\subsubsection{Koopmans' theorem}

Koopmans' theorem estimates removal and attachment energies while keeping the
HF orbitals frozen. The ground-state energy of the $N$-electron system is
\begin{equation}
  {}^N E_0=\sum_i h_{ii}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\mel{ij}{}{ij}.
\end{equation}
Removing the electron from occupied spin orbital $k$ gives
\begin{equation}
  {}^{N-1}E_k=\sum_{i\ne k}h_{ii}
  +\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\ne k}\sum_{j\ne k}\mel{ij}{}{ij}.
\end{equation}
The frozen-orbital ionization potential is therefore
\begin{align}
  \text{IP}&\approx{}^{N-1}E_k-{}^N E_0
  \\
  &=-\mel{k}{\hh}{k}
    -\frac{1}{2}\sum_i\mel{ik}{}{ik}
    -\frac{1}{2}\sum_j\mel{kj}{}{kj}
  \\
  &=-\mel{k}{\hh}{k}-\sum_i\mel{ik}{}{ik}
  =\boxed{-\epsilon_k}.
\end{align}
Similarly, attachment to vacant spin orbital $c$ gives
\begin{align}
  \text{EA}&\approx{}^N E_0-{}^{N+1}E^c
  \\
  &=-\mel{c}{\hh}{c}-\sum_i\mel{ci}{}{ci}
  =\boxed{-\epsilon_c}.
\end{align}

Koopmans' estimate is usually more accurate for ionization potentials than
for electron affinities. For ionization,
\begin{equation}
  \text{IP}=-\epsilon_i
  +\underbrace{\Delta_\text{relax}^{N-1}}_{\blue{<0}}
  +\underbrace{\Delta_\text{corr}}_{\red{>0}},
\end{equation}
so orbital relaxation and correlation partially cancel. For attachment,
\begin{equation}
  \text{EA}=-\epsilon_a
  +\underbrace{\Delta_\text{relax}^{N+1}}_{\blue{>0}}
  +\underbrace{\Delta_\text{corr}}_{\red{>0}},
\end{equation}
and the corrections have the same sign.

The $N$-electron Hartree--Fock Hamiltonian is the sum of the one-electron
Fock operators,
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\chH^\text{HF}}_{N\text{-electron operator}}
  =\sum_{i=1}^{N}\underbrace{\hf(\bx_i)}_{\text{one-electron operator}}
  =\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\hh(\bx_i)+\Hat{v}^\text{HF}(\bx_i)\right].
\end{equation}
Although
\begin{equation}
  \chH^\text{HF}\ket{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  =\left(\sum_i\epsilon_i\right)\ket{\Psi_\text{HF}},
\end{equation}
the eigenvalue is \red{not} the HF energy. Nor is the HF determinant, in
general, an eigenfunction of the electronic Hamiltonian. Instead,
\begin{equation}
  \chH_\text{elec}\ket{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  \ne E_\text{HF}\ket{\Psi_\text{HF}},
  \qquad
  \mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\chH_\text{elec}}{\Psi_\text{HF}}=E_\text{HF}.
\end{equation}

The sum of the orbital energies counts every electron pair twice,
\begin{equation}
  \sum_i\epsilon_i=\sum_i h_{ii}+\sum_{ij}(\cJ_{ij}-\cK_{ij}),
\end{equation}
whereas the HF energy contains the required factor $1/2$,
\begin{equation}
  E_\text{HF}=\sum_i h_{ii}
  +\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}(\cJ_{ij}-\cK_{ij}).
\end{equation}
It follows that
\begin{equation}
  \mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{
  \sum_{i<j}r_{ij}^{-1}-\sum_i v^\text{HF}(\bx_i)
  }{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  =-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}(\cJ_{ij}-\cK_{ij}).
\end{equation}
This is the first-order correction in \red{M\o ller--Plesset perturbation
theory}.

\subsection{The Roothaan--Hall equations}
\subsubsection{The basis-set approximation}

The molecular orbitals are expanded in a finite set of $K$ basis functions,
\begin{equation}
  \psi_i(\br)=\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}C_{\mu i}\phi_\mu(\br),
  \qquad
  \ket{i}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}C_{\mu i}\ket{\mu}.
\end{equation}
Substitution into the canonical HF equation gives
\begin{equation}
  \hf\sum_\nu C_{\nu i}\ket{\nu}
  =\epsilon_i\sum_\nu C_{\nu i}\ket{\nu}.
\end{equation}
Projection from the left with $\bra{\mu}$ yields
\begin{equation}
  \sum_\nu F_{\mu\nu}C_{\nu i}
  =\sum_\nu S_{\mu\nu}C_{\nu i}\epsilon_i,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  F_{\mu\nu}=\mel{\mu}{\hf}{\nu},
  \qquad
  S_{\mu\nu}=\braket{\mu}{\nu}.
\end{equation}
Collecting all orbitals gives the \red{Roothaan--Hall equations}
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\bF\bC=\bS\bC\bE}.
  \label{eq:roothaan-hall}
\end{equation}
The coefficient matrix $\bC$ contains the molecular-orbital coefficients,
while the diagonal matrix $\bE$ contains the orbital energies,
\begin{equation}
  \bC=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    C_{11}&C_{12}&\cdots&C_{1K}\\
    C_{21}&C_{22}&\cdots&C_{2K}\\
    \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
    C_{K1}&C_{K2}&\cdots&C_{KK}
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \bE=\operatorname{diag}(\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2,\ldots,\epsilon_K).
\end{equation}

Because the original basis is generally nonorthogonal, Eq.~\eqref{eq:roothaan-hall}
is a generalized eigenvalue problem with metric $\bS$. Introduce an
orthogonalizing matrix $\bX$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
  \bX^\dag\bS\bX=\bI,
  \qquad
  \bC=\bX\bC'.
\end{equation}
Left multiplication of Eq.~\eqref{eq:roothaan-hall} by $\bX^\dag$ gives
\begin{align}
  \bX^\dag\bF\bX\bC'
  &=\bX^\dag\bS\bX\bC'\bE
  \\
  \bF'\bC'&=\bC'\bE,
\end{align}
where $\bF'=\bX^\dag\bF\bX$. Thus orthogonalization converts the
generalized problem into a standard Hermitian eigenvalue problem.

\subsubsection{The Fock matrix}

Expansion of the occupied spin orbitals in the basis gives the AO Fock
matrix
\begin{align}
  F_{\mu\nu}
  &=\mel{\mu}{\hh+\sum_i(\chJ_i-\chK_i)}{\nu}
  \\
  &=\underbrace{\mel{\mu}{\hh}{\nu}}_{H_{\mu\nu}}
  +\sum_i\left(
    \mel{\mu\chi_i}{r_{12}^{-1}}{\nu\chi_i}
    -\mel{\mu\chi_i}{r_{12}^{-1}}{\chi_i\nu}
  \right)
  \\
  &=H_{\mu\nu}
  +\sum_{\lambda\sigma}P_{\lambda\sigma}
    \left(\braket{\mu\sigma}{\nu\lambda}
    -\braket{\mu\sigma}{\lambda\nu}\right)
  \\
  &=H_{\mu\nu}
  +\underbrace{\sum_{\lambda\sigma}P_{\lambda\sigma}
    \mel{\mu\sigma}{}{\nu\lambda}}_{G_{\mu\nu}}.
\end{align}
Here
\begin{equation}
  H_{\mu\nu}=\mel{\mu}{\hh}{\nu},
  \qquad
  P_{\lambda\sigma}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}C_{\lambda i}C_{\sigma i}^*,
  \qquad
  F_{\mu\nu}=H_{\mu\nu}+G_{\mu\nu}.
\end{equation}
The Fock matrix therefore depends on the occupied-orbital coefficients through
the \red{density matrix} $\bP$. This dependence makes the Roothaan--Hall
equations nonlinear and motivates the self-consistent-field procedure.

\subsubsection{The Hartree--Fock energy}

Substituting the basis expansion into the spin-orbital energy expression gives
\begin{align}
  E_\text{HF}
  &=\sum_{\mu\nu}P_{\nu\mu}
    \left(H_{\mu\nu}
    +\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda\sigma}P_{\sigma\lambda}
      \mel{\mu\lambda}{}{\nu\sigma}\right)
  \\
  &=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu\nu}P_{\nu\mu}
    (H_{\mu\nu}+F_{\mu\nu})
  \\
  &=\boxed{\frac{1}{2}\Tr[\bP(\bH+\bF)]}.
  \label{eq:hf-energy-matrix}
\end{align}

For a closed-shell system, the density matrix is
\begin{equation}
  P_{\red{\mu\nu}}
  =2\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}C_{\red{\mu}i}C_{\red{\nu}i}^*,
  \qquad
  \boxed{\bP=2\bC_\text{o}\bC_\text{o}^\dag},
\end{equation}
where $\bC_\text{o}$ contains the occupied MO coefficients. In chemists'
notation, the closed-shell AO Fock matrix becomes
\begin{equation}
  F_{\red{\mu\nu}}=H_{\red{\mu\nu}}
  +\underbrace{\sum_{\blue{\lambda\sigma}}
    P_{\blue{\sigma\lambda}}
    \rbraket{\red{\mu\nu}}{\blue{\lambda\sigma}}}_{J_{\red{\mu\nu}}}
  -\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\blue{\lambda\sigma}}
    P_{\blue{\sigma\lambda}}
    \rbraket{\red{\mu}\blue{\sigma}}{\blue{\lambda}\red{\nu}}}
    _{K_{\red{\mu\nu}}}.
  \label{eq:rhf-fock-ao}
\end{equation}
The corresponding energy is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  E_\text{HF}={}&\sum_{\red{\mu\nu}}
    P_{\red{\nu\mu}}H_{\red{\mu\nu}}
  \\
  &+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\red{\mu\nu}\blue{\lambda\sigma}}
    P_{\red{\nu\mu}}
    \left[
      \rbraket{\red{\mu\nu}}{\blue{\lambda\sigma}}
      -\frac{1}{2}
       \rbraket{\red{\mu}\blue{\sigma}}{\blue{\lambda}\red{\nu}}
    \right]
    P_{\blue{\sigma\lambda}}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
