\section{Hartree--Fock theory}

\subsection{The electronic problem}
\subsubsection{Motivations}

We consider the \red{time-independent} Schr\"odinger equation and seek
stationary electronic states. Hartree--Fock (HF) theory provides an
\red{ab initio} description: apart from the choice of basis set, the method
contains no empirical parameters. Throughout this chapter, relativistic
effects are neglected.

The central approximation is a \red{mean-field} treatment of the
electron--electron interaction. Each electron moves in the average field
generated by all the others. This replacement does not remove the
electron--electron interaction, but it restricts the form in which that
interaction enters the wavefunction. Despite this restriction, HF theory is
the starting point for much of electronic-structure theory.

\subsubsection{The Born--Oppenheimer approximation}

For a molecule containing $N$ electrons and $M$ nuclei, the molecular
wavefunction depends on the electronic coordinates $\{\br_i\}$ and nuclear
coordinates $\{\bR_A\}$. It satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \chH \Phi(\{\br_i\},\{\bR_A\})
  = \cE \Phi(\{\br_i\},\{\bR_A\}).
\end{equation}
The total Hamiltonian contains five contributions,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \chH
  = \green{\chT_\text{n}}
  + \blue{\chT_\text{e}}
  + \orange{\chV_\text{ne}}
  + \red{\chV_\text{ee}}
  + \violet{\chV_\text{nn}}
  }.
  \label{eq:total-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
Here, $\green{\chT_\text{n}}$ and $\blue{\chT_\text{e}}$ are the nuclear
and electronic kinetic-energy operators. The operator
$\orange{\chV_\text{ne}}$ describes the Coulomb attraction between nuclei
and electrons, while $\red{\chV_\text{ee}}$ and
$\violet{\chV_\text{nn}}$ describe the electron--electron and
nucleus--nucleus Coulomb repulsions.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{coord}
  \caption{Electronic and nuclear coordinates entering the molecular
  Hamiltonian.}
  \label{fig:molecular-coordinates}
\end{figure}

In atomic units, for which $m=e=\hbar=1$, these terms read
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
  \green{\chT_\text{n}}
  &= -\sum_{A=1}^{M}\frac{\laplacian_A}{2M_A},
  &
  \blue{\chT_\text{e}}
  &= -\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\laplacian_i}{2},
  \\
  \orange{\chV_\text{ne}}
  &= -\sum_{A=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{Z_A}{r_{iA}},
  &
  \red{\chV_\text{ee}}
  &= \sum_{i<j}^{N}\frac{1}{r_{ij}},
  \\
  \violet{\chV_\text{nn}}
  &= \sum_{A<B}^{M}\frac{Z_AZ_B}{R_{AB}}.
\end{align}
\label{eq:hamiltonian-terms}
\end{subequations}
The symbol $\laplacian$ denotes the Laplace operator, $M_A$ and $Z_A$ are
the mass and charge of nucleus $A$, $r_{iA}$ is the distance between
electron $i$ and nucleus $A$, $r_{ij}$ is the distance between electrons
$i$ and $j$, and $R_{AB}$ is the distance between nuclei $A$ and $B$.

Because nuclear masses are much larger than the electron mass,
$M_A\gg 1$ in atomic units, the \red{Born--Oppenheimer approximation}
decouples electronic and nuclear motion. The electrons then move in the
field of fixed nuclei, and the molecular wavefunction is written as
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\{\br_i\},\{\bR_A\})
  = \Phi_\text{nucl}(\{\bR_A\})
    \Phi_\text{elec}(\{\br_i\},\{\bR_A\}).
\end{equation}
At a fixed nuclear geometry, the electronic factor satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \chH_\text{elec}\Phi_\text{elec}
  = \cE_\text{elec}\Phi_\text{elec},
  \qquad
  \boxed{
  \chH_\text{elec}
  = \blue{\chT_\text{e}}
  + \orange{\chV_\text{ne}}
  + \red{\chV_\text{ee}}
  }.
  \label{eq:electronic-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
The nuclear factor obeys
\begin{equation}
  \chH_\text{nucl}\Phi_\text{nucl}
  = \cE_\text{nucl}\Phi_\text{nucl},
  \qquad
  \boxed{
  \chH_\text{nucl}
  = \green{\chT_\text{n}}
  + \violet{\chV_\text{nn}}
  + \cE_\text{elec}(\{\bR_A\})
  }.
\end{equation}
Thus the nuclei move on the potential-energy surface defined by
$\violet{\chV_\text{nn}}+\cE_\text{elec}(\{\bR_A\})$. For a fixed nuclear
geometry, the corresponding total energy is
\begin{equation}
  \cE_\text{tot}
  = \cE_\text{elec}
  + \sum_{A<B}^{M}\frac{Z_AZ_B}{R_{AB}}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{The Pauli principle}

Electrons are spin-$1/2$ fermions. Their two spin functions are
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\blue{\alpha}}
  = \ket{\tfrac{1}{2},+\tfrac{1}{2}},
  \qquad
  \ket{\red{\beta}}
  = \ket{\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2}},
\end{equation}
corresponding respectively to spin-up and spin-down electrons. They are
orthonormal,
\begin{align}
  \braket{\blue{\alpha}}{\red{\beta}}
  &= \braket{\red{\beta}}{\blue{\alpha}}=0,
  &
  \braket{\blue{\alpha}}{\blue{\alpha}}
  &= \braket{\red{\beta}}{\red{\beta}}=1.
\end{align}
We combine the spin coordinate $\omega$ and spatial coordinate $\br$ into
the composite coordinate
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\violet{\bx}=(\orange{\omega},\red{\br})}.
\end{equation}

Indistinguishability requires the probability density to remain unchanged
when two particle labels are exchanged. For two particles,
\begin{equation}
  \abs{\Psi(\bx_1,\bx_2)}^2
  = \abs{\Psi(\bx_2,\bx_1)}^2,
\end{equation}
so the two wavefunctions may differ only by a phase,
\begin{equation}
  \Psi(\bx_2,\bx_1)=e^{\ii\theta}\Psi(\bx_1,\bx_2).
\end{equation}
Applying the permutation operator $\chP_{12}$ twice restores the original
ordering. Consequently $e^{2\ii\theta}=1$, and therefore
$e^{\ii\theta}=\pm1$. Bosonic wavefunctions take the positive sign, whereas
fermionic wavefunctions take the negative sign. The electronic wavefunction
must therefore satisfy the Pauli antisymmetry condition
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\bx_1,\ldots,\bx_i,\ldots,\bx_j,\ldots,\bx_N)
  =-
  \Phi(\bx_1,\ldots,\bx_j,\ldots,\bx_i,\ldots,\bx_N).
  \label{eq:pauli-antisymmetry}
\end{equation}
For two electrons at the same point, this relation gives
$\Psi(\bx,\bx)=-\Psi(\bx,\bx)$ and hence
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\Psi(\bx,\bx)=0}.
\end{equation}
The vanishing probability of finding two same-spin electrons at the same
point is the origin of the Fermi, or exchange, hole.

Given two one-electron functions $\chi_1(\bx)$ and $\chi_2(\bx)$, the
normalized antisymmetric two-electron wavefunction is
\begin{align}
  \Psi(\bx_1,\bx_2)
  &=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
    \begin{vmatrix}
      \chi_1(\bx_1)&\chi_2(\bx_1)\\
      \chi_1(\bx_2)&\chi_2(\bx_2)
    \end{vmatrix}
  \\
  &=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
    \left[
      \chi_1(\bx_1)\chi_2(\bx_2)
      -\chi_2(\bx_1)\chi_1(\bx_2)
    \right].
\end{align}
This construction is a \red{Slater determinant}. By contrast, the simple
product
\begin{equation}
  \Psi(\bx_1,\bx_2)=\chi_1(\bx_1)\chi_2(\bx_2)
\end{equation}
is a \green{Hartree product} and does not possess the required antisymmetry.

\subsubsection{The Hartree--Fock wavefunction}

Hartree--Fock theory approximates the $N$-electron wavefunction by a single
Slater determinant,
\begin{align}
  \Psi_\text{HF}(\bx_1,\ldots,\bx_N)
  &=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}
  \begin{vmatrix}
    \chi_1(\bx_1)&\chi_2(\bx_1)&\cdots&\chi_N(\bx_1)\\
    \chi_1(\bx_2)&\chi_2(\bx_2)&\cdots&\chi_N(\bx_2)\\
    \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
    \chi_1(\bx_N)&\chi_2(\bx_N)&\cdots&\chi_N(\bx_N)
  \end{vmatrix}
  \\
  &\equiv \ket{\chi_1\chi_2\cdots\chi_N}
  =\blue{\chA}\left[
    \chi_1(\bx_1)\chi_2(\bx_2)\cdots\chi_N(\bx_N)
  \right].
  \label{eq:hf-determinant}
\end{align}
The operator $\blue{\chA}$ is the antisymmetrizer. It converts the Hartree
product into an antisymmetric many-electron wavefunction.

Each spin orbital separates into spin and spatial factors,
\begin{equation}
  \chi_p(\bx)
  =\red{\sigma(\omega)}\blue{\psi_p^\sigma(\br)},
  \qquad
  \sigma(\omega)\in\{\alpha(\omega),\beta(\omega)\},
\end{equation}
and its spatial part is expanded as a linear combination of $K$ basis
functions,
\begin{equation}
  \blue{\psi_p^\sigma(\br)}
  =\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}C_{\mu p}^{\sigma}\green{\phi_\mu(\br)}.
  \label{eq:lcao-introduction}
\end{equation}
The spin orbitals are orthonormal,
\begin{equation}
  \braket{\chi_p}{\chi_q}=\delta_{pq},
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{pq}$ is the Kronecker delta. The underlying basis functions
need not be orthogonal. Their overlaps define the matrix elements
\begin{equation}
  \braket{\phi_\mu}{\phi_\nu}=S_{\mu\nu}.
\end{equation}

In restricted Hartree--Fock theory, which describes closed-shell systems,
the $\alpha$ and $\beta$ electrons in a pair share the same spatial orbital,
\begin{equation}
  \chi_p^\text{RHF}(\bx)
  =
  \begin{cases}
    \blue{\alpha(\omega)}\psi_p(\br),\\
    \red{\beta(\omega)}\psi_p(\br).
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The closed-shell RHF ansatz does not describe open-shell systems. In
unrestricted Hartree--Fock theory, the two spin components may instead use
different spatial orbitals,
\begin{equation}
  \chi_p^\text{UHF}(\bx)
  =
  \begin{cases}
    \alpha(\omega)\blue{\psi_p^\alpha(\br)},\\
    \beta(\omega)\red{\psi_p^\beta(\br)}.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This additional freedom permits the treatment of open-shell systems, but it
may break spin symmetry. Restricted open-shell Hartree--Fock theory retains
shared spatial orbitals, but the slides do not discuss that formalism.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{RHF_UHF}
  \caption{Restricted and unrestricted choices for the spatial parts of the
  spin orbitals.}
  \label{fig:rhf-uhf-orbitals}
\end{figure}

Restricted spatial orbitals satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \braket{\psi_p}{\psi_q}=\delta_{pq}.
\end{equation}
For UHF orbitals, orthogonality holds separately within each spin sector,
\begin{equation}
  \braket{\psi_p^\alpha}{\psi_q^\alpha}=\delta_{pq},
  \qquad
  \braket{\psi_p^\beta}{\psi_q^\beta}=\delta_{pq},
\end{equation}
whereas opposite-spin spatial orbitals need not be orthogonal,
\begin{equation}
  \braket{\psi_p^\alpha}{\psi_q^\beta}=S_{pq}^{\alpha\beta}.
\end{equation}

We use Greek indices $\mu,\nu,\ldots$ for the $K$ basis functions
$\{\phi_\mu\}$. General spatial-orbital and spin-orbital indices are
$p,q,r,s,\ldots$. The $K$ basis functions generate $K$ spatial orbitals and
$2K$ spin orbitals. Among the latter, $N$ are occupied and carry indices
$i,j,k,l,\ldots$, while the remaining $2K-N$ vacant orbitals carry indices
$a,b,c,d,\ldots$. A system with two electrons in each occupied spatial
orbital is closed shell. Otherwise it is open shell. For the ground state of
a closed-shell system, the first $N/2$ spatial orbitals are doubly occupied
and the remaining $K-N/2$ spatial orbitals are vacant.

\subsection{The Hartree--Fock approximation}
\subsubsection{The Hartree--Fock energy}

For fixed nuclei, the Hartree--Fock energy is the expectation value of the
electronic Hamiltonian over the normalized Slater determinant,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\EHF=\mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\chH_\text{elec}}{\Psi_\text{HF}}},
  \qquad
  \chH_\text{elec}=\blue{\chT_\text{e}}+\orange{\chV_\text{ne}}
  +\red{\chV_\text{ee}}.
\end{equation}
It is useful to separate this Hamiltonian into one- and two-electron parts,
\begin{equation}
  \chH_\text{elec}=\chO_1+\chO_2
  =\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hh(\bx_i)+\sum_{i<j}^{N}\frac{1}{r_{ij}},
\end{equation}
where the one-electron, or core, Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
  \hh(\bx_i)=-\frac{\laplacian_i}{2}
  -\sum_{A=1}^{M}\frac{Z_A}{r_{iA}},
  \qquad
  \chO_1=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hh(\bx_i),
\end{equation}
and the electron--electron repulsion is
\begin{equation}
  \chO_2=\red{\chV_\text{ee}}=\sum_{i<j}^{N}\frac{1}{r_{ij}}.
\end{equation}

The Slater--Condon rules reduce the one-electron expectation value to a sum
over occupied spin orbitals,
\begin{equation}
  \mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\chO_1}{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  =\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mel{\chi_i}{\hh}{\chi_i}
  =\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{ii}.
\end{equation}
The nuclear repulsion does not depend on the electronic coordinates. For a
normalized electronic wavefunction its expectation value is simply
$\violet{V_\text{nn}}$.

The two-electron expectation value contains a \green{Coulomb} contribution
and a \red{exchange} contribution for each occupied pair,
\begin{align}
  \mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\chO_2}{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  &=\sum_{i<j}^{N}(\cJ_{ij}-\cK_{ij})
  \\
  &=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}
    (\cJ_{ij}-\cK_{ij}).
\end{align}
The second form follows because $\cJ_{ii}=\cK_{ii}$, so the apparent
self-interaction cancels exactly. Combining the one- and two-electron terms
gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \EHF=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{ii}
  +\sum_{i<j}^{N}(\cJ_{ij}-\cK_{ij})
  }.
  \label{eq:hf-energy-spin-orbitals}
\end{equation}

The Coulomb operator generated by occupied spin orbital $\chi_j$ acts on
$\chi_i$ as
\begin{equation}
  \chJ_j(\bx_1)\ket{\chi_i(\bx_1)}
  =\left[
    \int\chi_j^*(\bx_2)\frac{1}{r_{12}}\chi_j(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_2}
  \right]\ket{\chi_i(\bx_1)}.
\end{equation}
Its expectation value is
\begin{align}
  \cJ_{ij}
  &=\mel{\chi_i(\bx_1)}{\chJ_j(\bx_1)}{\chi_i(\bx_1)}
  \\
  &=\iint \chi_i^*(\bx_1)\chi_j^*(\bx_2)\frac{1}{r_{12}}
    \chi_i(\bx_1)\chi_j(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_1}\dd{\bx_2}.
\end{align}
The Coulomb operator is local: after integration over $\bx_2$, it multiplies
$\chi_i(\bx_1)$ by a function of $\bx_1$.

The exchange operator instead acts according to
\begin{equation}
  \chK_j(\bx_1)\ket{\chi_i(\bx_1)}
  =\left[
    \int\chi_j^*(\bx_2)\frac{1}{r_{12}}\chi_i(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_2}
  \right]\ket{\chi_j(\bx_1)}.
\end{equation}
It is therefore nonlocal. Its matrix element is
\begin{align}
  \cK_{ij}
  &=\mel{\chi_i(\bx_1)}{\chK_j(\bx_1)}{\chi_i(\bx_1)}
  \\
  &=\iint \chi_i^*(\bx_1)\chi_j^*(\bx_2)\frac{1}{r_{12}}
    \chi_j(\bx_1)\chi_i(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_1}\dd{\bx_2}.
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Integrals}

We use both physicists' and chemists' notations. A one-electron integral is
written equivalently as
\begin{equation}
  \mel{i}{\hh}{j}=h_{ij}=\mel{\chi_i}{\hh}{\chi_j}
  =\int\chi_i^*(\bx_1)\hh(\bx_1)\chi_j(\bx_1)\dd{\bx_1}.
\end{equation}
In physicists' notation, an electron-repulsion integral (ERI) is
\begin{equation}
  \braket{ij}{kl}
  =\iint\chi_i^*(\bx_1)\chi_j^*(\bx_2)\frac{1}{r_{12}}
  \chi_k(\bx_1)\chi_l(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_1}\dd{\bx_2},
\end{equation}
and the corresponding antisymmetrized integral is
\begin{equation}
  \mel{ij}{}{kl}=\braket{ij}{kl}-\braket{ij}{lk}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the factor $1-\chP_{12}$ may be applied to the ket product.
In chemists' notation,
\begin{equation}
  \rbraket{ij}{kl}
  =\iint\chi_i^*(\bx_1)\chi_j(\bx_1)\frac{1}{r_{12}}
  \chi_k^*(\bx_2)\chi_l(\bx_2)\dd{\bx_1}\dd{\bx_2}.
\end{equation}
The same notation will be used for integrals over spin orbitals and spatial
orbitals; the surrounding context specifies which type is intended.

For complex-valued orbitals, physicists' ERIs obey
\begin{equation}
  \braket{ij}{kl}=\braket{ji}{lk}
  =\braket{kl}{ij}^*=\braket{lk}{ji}^*.
\end{equation}
For real-valued orbitals this becomes the eightfold symmetry
\begin{align}
  \braket{ij}{kl}
  &=\braket{ji}{lk}=\braket{kl}{ij}=\braket{lk}{ji}
  \\
  &=\braket{kj}{il}=\braket{jk}{li}=\braket{il}{kj}
  =\braket{li}{jk}.
\end{align}
In chemists' notation, the same real-valued symmetry is
\begin{align}
  \rbraket{ij}{kl}
  &=\rbraket{ji}{kl}=\rbraket{ij}{lk}=\rbraket{ji}{lk}
  \\
  &=\rbraket{kl}{ij}=\rbraket{lk}{ij}=\rbraket{kl}{ji}
  =\rbraket{lk}{ji}.
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Examples}

\paragraph{Normalization of a two-electron determinant.}
Let $\chi_1$ and $\chi_2$ be normalized orthogonal spin orbitals. The
two-electron determinant is
\begin{equation}
  \Psi_\text{HF}
  =\frac{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)-\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}{\sqrt{2}}.
\end{equation}
Expanding its norm gives
\begin{align}
  \braket{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  =\frac{1}{2}\big[&
  \braket{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)}{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)}
  -\braket{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)}{\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}
  \nonumber\\[-0.2em]
  &-\braket{\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}{\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)}
  +\braket{\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}{\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)}\big].
\end{align}
Product-state overlaps factor into one-electron overlaps. Orthogonality makes
the two cross terms vanish, while normalization makes each diagonal term
unity. Hence $\braket{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\Psi_\text{HF}}=(1-0-0+1)/2=1$.

\paragraph{One-electron energy of a two-electron determinant.}
For $\chO_1=\hh(1)+\hh(2)$, expansion over the two determinant products gives
\begin{align}
  \mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{\chO_1}{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  &=\frac{1}{2}\left[h_{11}+h_{22}-0-0+h_{22}+h_{11}\right]
  \\
  &=h_{11}+h_{22}.
\end{align}
The cross terms vanish because the operator acts on only one electron and
the remaining orbital overlap is zero.

\paragraph{Electron interaction in a two-electron determinant.}
Here $\chO_2=r_{12}^{-1}$. Direct expansion yields
\begin{align}
  \mel{\Psi_\text{HF}}{r_{12}^{-1}}{\Psi_\text{HF}}
  &=\frac{1}{2}\left[
    \cJ_{12}-\cK_{12}-\cK_{12}+\cJ_{12}
  \right]
  \\
  &=\cJ_{12}-\cK_{12}.
\end{align}
Together with the one-electron contribution, the energy is
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{E_\text{HF}=h_{11}+h_{22}+\cJ_{12}-\cK_{12}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Three-electron system.}
For occupied spin orbitals $\chi_1$, $\chi_2$, and $\chi_3$,
\begin{equation}
  \chO_1=\hh(\bx_1)+\hh(\bx_2)+\hh(\bx_3),
  \qquad
  \chO_2=r_{12}^{-1}+r_{13}^{-1}+r_{23}^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Applying the preceding result to every occupied pair gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  E_\text{HF}=h_{11}+h_{22}+h_{33}
  +\cJ_{12}+\cJ_{13}+\cJ_{23}
  -\cK_{12}-\cK_{13}-\cK_{23}
  }.
\end{equation}
